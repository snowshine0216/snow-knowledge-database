# Chapter 7 — Unsupervised Learning

> [!abstract]+ Chapter at a glance
> Finding structure when there's **no labeled outcome** — for exploration, for feature creation, or as a preprocessing step before supervised modeling. The chapter covers **dimension reduction** (**PCA**) and three families of **clustering** (**K-means**, **hierarchical**, **model-based**), then closes on the unglamorous-but-decisive issues of **scaling** and **mixed/categorical data** that quietly determine whether any of it works. Because these methods are **distance-based**, preprocessing isn't optional — it's the lever that most affects the result.

## Core concepts

**Principal Components Analysis (PCA)**
- Finds new orthogonal axes — **principal components** — that are linear combinations of the original variables capturing **maximum variance**. The first component captures the most, the second the most of what's left, and so on.
- **Loadings** describe how each original variable contributes to a component (and let you interpret what it "means"). A **scree plot** shows variance explained per component, guiding how many to keep.
- Primary uses: **dimension reduction**, denoising, and visualization. **Correspondence analysis** is the analog for categorical data.

**K-means clustering**
- Partitions records into **K** clusters, each represented by a **centroid** (mean), minimizing the **within-cluster sum of squares** (total squared distance of points to their centroid). Iterative: assign points to nearest centroid, recompute centroids, repeat.
- You must **choose K** (the **elbow** method — diminishing returns in within-cluster SS — is the common heuristic). Fast and scalable, but assumes roughly **spherical, similar-size** clusters and is sensitive to initialization and scaling.

**Hierarchical clustering**
- Builds a **dendrogram** (tree) by **agglomerative** merging: start with each point as its own cluster and repeatedly merge the closest pair under a **dissimilarity** measure and a **linkage** rule — **complete** (max distance), **single** (min), **average**, or **Ward** (minimizes variance increase).
- No need to pre-specify K (you cut the tree at a chosen height), and the dendrogram is **interpretable**, but it **doesn't scale** to very large datasets.

**Model-based clustering**
- Assumes the data is generated by a **mixture of (multivariate) normal** distributions; fits the mixture and assigns probabilities of cluster membership. Uses a likelihood criterion like **BIC** to choose the **number and shape** of clusters. More principled and flexible about cluster shape/size, but assumption-heavy and computationally heavier.

**Scaling and categorical variables (the decisive preprocessing)**
- Clustering and PCA are **distance/variance based**, so **unscaled** variables let large-magnitude features dominate. **Standardization** (z-scores) is usually **required**, not optional.
- **Dominant variables**: a single high-variance feature can hijack PCA/clustering — inspect and rescale.
- **Mixed data**: Euclidean distance is undefined for categories; use **Gower's distance** for mixed numeric/categorical data. **Pure categorical** clustering is genuinely problematic and needs careful, purpose-built measures.

## Quiz

**1.** What does **PCA** do, and how do **loadings** and a **scree plot** help you use it?

> [!example]- Show answer
> **PCA** rotates the data onto new orthogonal axes — **principal components** — ordered by how much **variance** they capture, with each component a linear combination of the original variables. This concentrates the signal into the first few components, enabling **dimension reduction** and visualization. **Loadings** tell you how much each original variable contributes to a component, so you can **interpret** what a component represents (e.g. "overall size" or "a contrast between A and B"). A **scree plot** graphs variance explained per component; the "elbow" where it flattens tells you **how many components to keep** to retain most information with far fewer dimensions.

**2.** Describe the **K-means** algorithm and its objective.

> [!example]- Show answer
> **K-means** partitions data into **K** clusters to minimize the **within-cluster sum of squares** — the total squared distance from each point to its cluster's **centroid** (mean). It iterates: (1) initialize K centroids, (2) **assign** each point to its nearest centroid, (3) **recompute** each centroid as the mean of its assigned points, (4) repeat until assignments stop changing. It's fast and scales well, but converges to a **local** optimum (so it's run with multiple random starts) and implicitly assumes roughly spherical, comparably sized clusters.

**3.** How do you choose **K**, and why is it inherently somewhat subjective?

> [!example]- Show answer
> The common heuristic is the **elbow method**: plot within-cluster SS (or another quality measure) against K and look for the "elbow" where adding clusters yields **diminishing returns**. Other aids include silhouette scores and, for model-based clustering, **BIC**. It's subjective because more clusters **always** reduce within-cluster SS (K = n gives zero), so there's no purely mechanical optimum — you're balancing fit against parsimony and **interpretability**. Domain knowledge ("we expect ~4 customer segments") often decides as much as the metric. Clustering finds structure you must still judge as meaningful.

**4.** Contrast **hierarchical** clustering with **K-means**. When is each preferable?

> [!example]- Show answer
> **K-means** requires you to fix **K** up front, produces a flat partition, and **scales** to large data. **Hierarchical** (agglomerative) clustering builds a full **dendrogram** by merging closest clusters, needs **no preset K** (you cut the tree where you like), and is **interpretable** as a nested structure — but it's **O(n²)**-ish and doesn't scale. Prefer **K-means** for large datasets where you have a rough K in mind; prefer **hierarchical** for smaller datasets where you want to *see* the nesting and decide the number of clusters from the tree, or when clusters have a natural hierarchy (e.g. taxonomy).

**5.** What is a **linkage** method, and how do complete, single, and Ward linkage differ?

> [!example]- Show answer
> **Linkage** defines the distance between two **clusters** (not just two points) when deciding what to merge. **Complete** linkage uses the **maximum** pairwise distance → compact, similarly-sized clusters, sensitive to outliers. **Single** linkage uses the **minimum** distance → can "chain" points into long straggly clusters (good for non-globular shapes, bad for noise). **Ward** linkage merges the pair that **least increases within-cluster variance** → tends to produce balanced, spherical clusters and is a popular default. The choice noticeably changes the dendrogram, so it's a real modeling decision.

**6.** What does **model-based clustering** assume, and what does it buy you over K-means?

> [!example]- Show answer
> It assumes the data is a **mixture of multivariate normal distributions** — each cluster is a Gaussian "blob" with its own mean and **covariance** (shape/orientation/size). Fitting the mixture buys: (1) clusters that can be **elliptical and different-sized**, not just K-means' spheres; (2) **soft** assignments (probability of belonging to each cluster); and (3) a principled way to **choose the number of clusters** via **BIC**. The cost is stronger distributional assumptions and heavier computation. Use it when K-means' spherical assumption is clearly wrong and you can defend a Gaussian-mixture model.

**7.** Why is **standardization** so consequential for PCA and clustering — more so than for many supervised methods?

> [!example]- Show answer
> Because PCA and clustering are driven directly by **variance and distance**, and both are dominated by whichever variable has the **largest numeric scale**. Unscaled, "annual revenue in dollars" will swamp "satisfaction on a 1–5 scale" in every distance and every principal component, regardless of true relevance — so the clusters/components just reflect the units you happened to use. **Standardizing** (z-scores) puts variables on equal footing so structure reflects **patterns, not measurement scales**. Many supervised methods (trees) are scale-invariant, but these unsupervised, geometry-based methods are not — preprocessing *is* the analysis here.

**8.** How do you cluster data that mixes numeric and categorical variables?

> [!example]- Show answer
> Standard Euclidean distance is undefined for categories, so use a distance designed for **mixed types** — **Gower's distance** — which computes a per-variable similarity appropriate to each type (range-normalized difference for numerics, match/no-match for categoricals) and combines them into one dissimilarity. You then feed that distance matrix to a distance-based clusterer (e.g. hierarchical). The book also cautions that **purely categorical** clustering is genuinely hard and that mixed-type clustering needs care — naively one-hot-encoding categories and running K-means often gives misleading geometry.

**9.** *(Applied)* You want to cluster customers described by spend (\$), age, region (categorical), and plan tier (ordinal). Outline a sound approach.

> [!example]- Show answer
> (1) **Inspect and preprocess**: standardize the numeric variables (spend, age) — note spend is likely **right-skewed**, so consider a **log transform** first so one whale doesn't dominate. (2) **Handle the categoricals**: region is nominal, plan tier is ordinal (could be treated as numeric or kept categorical). (3) Because the data is **mixed-type**, compute **Gower's distance** rather than forcing Euclidean. (4) Cluster with a distance-based method (**hierarchical** to read a dendrogram and pick the number of clusters, or a method that accepts a distance matrix). (5) **Choose the number of clusters** via the dendrogram/elbow plus business interpretability, then **profile** each cluster to confirm the segments are meaningful and actionable. Watch for a **dominant variable** silently driving the solution.

**10.** *(Applied)* A stakeholder asks, "Which clustering is *correct*?" How do you respond?

> [!example]- Show answer
> There's no ground-truth "correct" clustering in unsupervised learning — by definition there are **no labels** to be right or wrong against. Different methods, distance metrics, scalings, and values of K all produce different, internally valid groupings. The right questions are instead: **Is it stable?** (does it hold under resampling/different seeds?), **Is it interpretable?** (do the clusters correspond to describable, distinct profiles?), and **Is it useful?** (does acting on the segments improve a downstream decision or metric?). You validate clusters by **usefulness and stability**, not correctness — and you make the preprocessing choices (scaling, distance, K) explicit because they, not some hidden truth, shaped the result.

## Deeper understanding (expansion)

> [!info]+ 💡 In unsupervised learning, preprocessing *is* the model
> In supervised learning you can lean on a target to tell you when you've gone wrong — a bad scaling shows up as worse test accuracy. Unsupervised methods have **no such safety net**: there's no label to penalize a poor choice, so every decision about **scaling, transformation, distance metric, and K** silently *becomes* part of the result. Cluster the same customers in raw dollars vs. log-standardized dollars and you get **different customers grouped together** — and nothing flags one as wrong. This is why the chapter spends its final, most practical pages on scaling and Gower's distance rather than fancier algorithms: with unsupervised learning, the leverage is in the preprocessing, and "which algorithm" matters less than "what geometry did I impose on the data."

> [!info]+ 💡 PCA and clustering answer different questions — and pair well
> It's easy to lump all "unsupervised" methods together, but **PCA** asks *"along which axes does the data vary?"* (continuous structure, dimension reduction) while **clustering** asks *"which records group together?"* (discrete structure, segmentation). They're complementary and frequently chained: run **PCA first** to denoise and compress to a handful of informative components, **then cluster** in that reduced space — which removes scale/noise problems, speeds up clustering, and often produces cleaner, more separable groups. Recognizing they answer different questions keeps you from using one when you needed the other (e.g. reaching for clusters when you really wanted to know your data's intrinsic dimensionality).

> [!info]+ 💡 Clustering is hypothesis generation, not truth discovery
> Because there's no objective function tied to reality, clustering is best treated as a **structured way to form hypotheses** about your data, not as a machine that outputs *the* segments. The danger is **reification** — taking "Cluster 3" as a real, fixed kind of customer when it's an artifact of K, the distance metric, and the scaling you chose. The disciplines that keep it honest mirror the rest of the book: check **stability** (re-run with different seeds, subsamples, and K — do the same groupings recur, echoing Chapter 2's resampling mindset?), demand **interpretability** (can you describe and name each cluster?), and ultimately validate by **downstream usefulness**. A clustering earns trust the same way a model does — by proving it generalizes and drives better decisions, not by looking tidy.
